EDUCATIONAL CLINICIAN-REVIEW DRAFT

Vestibular Migraine Rehabilitation

A graded exercise library with schematic animations. Select exercises according to the person's assessed impairments and symptom tolerance—not every exercise is appropriate for every patient.

Clinical safety notice: This site is not a diagnostic tool or a personal prescription. Use with a clinician experienced in vestibular rehabilitation and migraine. The published evidence supports potential benefit, but VM protocols and dosing vary and evidence quality remains limited. Do not begin a new programme during a severe/unusual attack without clinician advice.

Before starting

  • Confirm vestibular migraine diagnosis and consider other causes (including BPPV, vestibular hypofunction, medication effects, and neurological/medical causes).
  • Screen falls risk, neck pain/range, visual-motion sensitivity, hearing symptoms, migraine pattern, medications and relevant comorbidities.
  • Record baseline symptoms and function. Clinicians may consider DHI, ABC, gait/balance measures and a symptom/attack diary where appropriate.
  • Choose only the modules that match assessment findings. Start seated or near a stable support when needed.

The animations are simplified schematic demonstrations, not motion-capture or biomechanically validated videos. A clinician should demonstrate the actual movement and correct technique.

Gaze / head-eye

1. VOR X1 — stationary target

Purpose: Train gaze stability when head movement causes visual blurring, if assessment indicates this is appropriate.

How to do it

  1. Sit in a supportive chair. Place a small letter or cross at eye level, about an arm's length away.
  2. Keep eyes on the target while turning the head a small, comfortable amount left and right.
  3. Repeat with gentle up/down nods only if tolerated and prescribed.
Initial dose: clinician-set short bouts (for example, 10–20 seconds), with rest between bouts. Increase duration or speed—not both at once—only if symptoms recover promptly.

Stop or reduce speed/range if the target cannot be kept clear, symptoms escalate, or recovery is prolonged.

Gaze / head-eye

2. VOR X2 — moving target

Purpose: Higher-demand gaze-stability training, only when indicated and VOR X1 is well tolerated.

How to do it

  1. Begin seated with a clear target in view.
  2. Move the target slowly in one direction while turning the head in the opposite direction.
  3. Maintain visual fixation. Keep the movement small and controlled.
Dose: clinician-prescribed; this is an advanced option, not a default starting exercise.

Do not start this exercise unsupervised if VOR X1 provokes significant symptoms or if you have prominent visual-motion sensitivity.

Gaze / head-eye

3. Eye–head shifts between two targets

Purpose: Practice coordinated gaze shifts or substitution strategies when selected by a clinician.

How to do it

  1. Place two large, stationary targets at comfortable eye level, separated by a modest distance.
  2. Look at one target, then shift the eyes to the other.
  3. Turn the head comfortably to align with the new target, keeping the movement controlled.
Initial dose: a few slow repetitions, seated; rest as needed.

Do not use isolated eye movements as a replacement for gaze-stability training when the goal is VOR adaptation; this module targets coordinated eye–head shifts.

Habituation

4. Graded head turns / habituation

Purpose: Gradually reduce sensitivity to a specific head/body movement that provokes mild, brief symptoms.

How to do it

  1. Choose one reproducible, safe movement identified during assessment.
  2. Start seated with a small range and slow pace.
  3. Pause until symptoms settle, then repeat only if the response remains manageable.
Dose: short, individually prescribed exposures; keep a note of the trigger and recovery time.

Do not deliberately provoke a major migraine, severe nausea, or prolonged worsening. Stop if the response is stronger than the agreed plan.

Visual-motion sensitivity

5. Visual-motion exposure (low intensity)

Purpose: Address visual-motion sensitivity when it is a prominent assessed impairment.

How to do it

  1. Use a simple, low-contrast, slow-moving pattern on a screen, viewed while seated.
  2. Keep exposure brief and at a comfortable viewing distance; avoid flashing imagery.
  3. Increase only one variable at a time (duration, speed, or visual complexity) under guidance.
Dose: clinician-guided and symptom-limited. No universal duration is established for all VM patients.

Avoid bright/high-intensity stimulation or immersive VR as a starting point. Stop for escalating headache, nausea, visual aura or lingering symptoms.

Balance

6. Supported standing / weight shifts

Purpose: Improve confidence and postural control when balance deficits are present.

How to do it

  1. Stand near a firm counter with feet comfortably apart and a chair behind if needed.
  2. Shift a small amount of weight left/right, then forward/back, without lifting the feet.
  3. Keep a hand near the support and stop before losing balance.
Initial dose: 20–30 seconds or a few controlled shifts, with rest. Increase only with safe technique.

Do not close the eyes, stand on foam, or narrow the base of support without a clinician's assessment and guarding.

Functional balance

7. Sit-to-stand practice

Purpose: Build tolerance for a daily functional transition, if safe and relevant.

How to do it

  1. Use a stable chair against a wall; sit with feet under or slightly behind knees.
  2. Lean forward from the hips and stand up slowly, using armrests if needed.
  3. Pause to regain steadiness, then sit down with control.
Initial dose: 3–5 repetitions with rest; reduce repetitions if symptoms or fatigue increase.

Have supervision if there is fall risk, leg weakness, or marked postural dizziness.

Gait / mobility

8. Walking progression

Purpose: Rebuild everyday mobility and endurance when walking is limited by dizziness or imbalance.

How to do it

  1. Walk on a clear, level route at a comfortable pace, ideally with another person if unsteady.
  2. Start with a short, manageable duration and rest before symptoms escalate.
  3. Progress duration first. Head turns while walking should be added only if prescribed and safe.
Initial dose: a few minutes as tolerated; the clinician should tailor the plan to fitness, falls risk and migraine pattern.
Relaxation adjunct

9. Comfortable paced breathing

Purpose: Support relaxation and coping; it is not a treatment for the vestibular cause itself.

How to do it

  1. Sit comfortably with shoulders relaxed.
  2. Breathe quietly through the nose if comfortable; allow the exhale to be easy and unforced.
  3. Continue for 1–3 minutes, stopping if light-headedness or air hunger occurs.
Dose: brief practice as comfortable, often during a calm part of the day.

Progression principles

  1. Match the impairment: VOR exercises for assessed gaze instability; habituation for reproducible motion-provoked symptoms; visual-motion exposure for visual sensitivity; balance/gait work for functional deficits.
  2. Start low: comfortable posture, small range, slow speed, short bouts, and rest breaks.
  3. Change one variable at a time: duration, speed, range, background complexity, posture, or task difficulty.
  4. Use recovery as a guide: symptoms should remain manageable and settle toward baseline promptly. If symptoms are escalating or persist substantially after exercise, reduce dose and consult the clinician.
  5. Review weekly or as agreed: symptoms, adherence, daily activity, falls/near-falls, migraine attacks and functional measures.

Do not copy dosing from peripheral vestibular hypofunction guidelines directly into VM. Those guidelines apply to a different population. A 2026 randomized trial used an individualized four-week programme with three daily home sessions of short repetitions and tailored intensity; its design is a useful example of individualisation, not a universal prescription.

Simple session log

This form stores nothing and sends no data. Use it as a note for the clinician; copy or print the result if desired.

Stop and seek help

  • Stop the exercise and contact the treating clinician if there is a substantial or persistent worsening, new falls/near-falls, or symptoms repeatedly trigger migraine attacks.
  • Seek urgent medical care for sudden severe headache, new weakness/numbness, slurred speech, double vision, fainting, chest pain, or sudden inability to stand or walk safely.
